# Script: A 10-protein blood panel that picks out ALS with high accuracy

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published in Nature Communications on October 19, 2026, by Woo Jae Kim, Eleanor Thomas, Jonathan Glass, and colleagues at Emory University in Atlanta. The title is: "Ten protein panel predicts ALS from plasma with high accuracy."

The question. There is no single test that diagnoses ALS. Doctors rely on history, examination, and nerve tests, and people often wait many months for an answer. The best-known blood marker, neurofilament light, or N-F-L, rises in ALS, but it also rises in other neurological conditions. The team asked: could a combination of blood proteins pick out ALS more accurately than neurofilament alone?

What they did. They measured more than three hundred proteins, linked to brain injury and to inflammation, in blood plasma from 632 people with ALS and 160 healthy controls, all from a single ALS center. The controls were spouses and care partners. A machine-learning method searched for the most useful combination of proteins. They then tested that panel in a separate group of 144 people with ALS and 37 controls.

What they found. The final panel has ten proteins. It includes neurofilament light and heavy, a form of tau called p-tau 181, and several inflammation-related and other proteins. In the separate validation group, the panel's balanced accuracy was about 98 percent, with only one person with ALS and one control misclassified. Neurofilament light alone reached about 90 percent. Age and sex added almost nothing. The panel did especially well for people who were hardest to call with neurofilament alone, often men with arm-onset ALS and milder disability. And in one striking case, a patient first diagnosed with ALS was flagged by the panel as not ALS. He was later found to have inclusion body myositis, a condition that can mimic ALS.

What it means. A blood test like this could one day help general neurologists and primary care doctors decide who should be sent quickly to an ALS specialist. That could shorten the long road to diagnosis and give people earlier access to care and clinical trials.

Honest caveats. The comparison was against healthy people, not against conditions that are easily confused with ALS, such as myasthenia gravis or inclusion body myositis. All samples came from one center, and blood was taken once, not over time. It is a research result, not a test you can order today.

What's next. The authors plan to test the panel against ALS mimics and other neurodegenerative diseases, at multiple centers, and to see whether it can track progression over time. This is educational only, not medical advice. Talk with your care team about your own situation.

# Script: A gut-microbiome transplant for ALS — a small trial with mixed signals

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on October 8, 2026, in the journal eBioMedicine. It's a clinical trial from four ALS centers in Italy, called FETR-ALS, led by the team of Mandrioli in Modena, with first author Zucchi. The title is: "Immune effects of faecal microbiota transplantation in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis."

Here's the question. Your gut is home to trillions of bacteria, and they help tune your immune system. In ALS, the immune system is out of balance. One group of immune cells, called regulatory T cells, or T-regs, act like peacekeepers that calm inflammation, and earlier studies linked lower T-reg levels to faster progression. So the team asked: if you replace someone's gut bacteria with bacteria from a healthy, screened donor, does that boost T-regs, and is it safe?

What did they do? They enrolled forty-two adults whose ALS had started within the last eighteen months and whose breathing was still fairly strong. Two out of every three got the real transplant. The rest got a sham, or fake, procedure, and participants didn't know which they had. The transplant was given twice, at the start and at six months, through a thin tube passed through the nose into the small intestine. The main goal was a rise in T-regs at six months.

What they found. The main goal was not met. Forty-four percent of the transplant group had a big rise in T-regs, versus twenty percent of the sham group, but with this few people, that difference could easily be chance. On average, though, T-reg levels did rise a bit more with the transplant. On the standard A-L-S functional rating scale, the sham group lost about one point a month, and the transplant group lost about three quarters of a point a month, a modest difference that was statistically significant. But a muscle strength score went the other way, slightly worse in the transplant group at six months. Side effects were mostly stomach or infection related, and there were no major safety concerns.

Why does this matter for sporadic ALS? It's a careful, sham-controlled first test of a gut-based approach to calming the immune system in ALS, and it suggests the procedure can be done safely. The slower decline on the rating scale is encouraging, but it was a secondary measure, in a small group.

Honest caveats. Only twenty-nine people finished the six-month follow-up. The main goal failed. The strength result pointed the opposite way. And the researchers call this exploratory, a proof of concept, not proof that it works. Please don't try do-it-yourself transplants, which carry real infection risk. And if you're in a clinical trial, check with your study team before any new treatment.

What's next? A larger, longer trial is needed to see whether gut transplants really slow ALS. That's tonight's paper. This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

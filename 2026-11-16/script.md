# Script: Does ALS build up in steps? Age patterns from the Australian MND Registry

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on November 14th, 2026, in the journal Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis and Frontotemporal Degeneration. It is peer reviewed and free to read. It comes from Steve Vucic, Matthew Kiernan, and colleagues at clinics across Australia, using the Australian Motor Neuron Disease Registry. The title is: "Power-law age-dependence supports phenotype-specific multistep architecture in MND."

The question. For most people, ALS has no single known cause. One long-standing idea is the multistep model: ALS appears only after a person builds up several hits over a lifetime, such as gene variants, exposures, and aging. If that's true, the risk of getting ALS should rise with age in a particular mathematical pattern called a power law. But a recent study argued the data fit a different pattern better, a simple exponential curve, where risk just keeps doubling every so many years. So this team asked: in Australian data, which pattern fits better?

What they did. They used registry records from 2005 to 2016, from 10 major Australian clinics. The main analysis included 2,556 people whose symptoms began between ages 30 and 74. The average age at onset was about 60. They counted new cases in five-year age bands, compared them with the size of the Australian population at each age, and tested both mathematical models head to head.

What they found. The power-law, multistep pattern fit clearly better than the exponential one, by a wide statistical margin. The estimate worked out to about 4.75 steps on average, in line with earlier studies that suggested roughly four to six. The number differed by where symptoms started: about 6 steps for bulbar onset, which starts with speech or swallowing, about 4.5 for leg onset, and about 3.7 for arm onset. The result held when they widened the age range, or limited it to people under 60.

What it means. This supports the view that sporadic ALS usually comes from several factors adding up over time, not one single cause, and that different forms of ALS may follow somewhat different paths. But it is a population-level pattern. It does not tell us what the steps are, and it does not mean every person goes through the same ones. It does not change diagnosis or treatment today.

Honest caveats. Curve fitting alone cannot prove how a disease starts. The registry does not capture every case, onset ages rely on memory, and no genetic test results were available. Two authors are co-inventors of a nerve-testing software tool, one also reports industry ties, and the study had no outside funding.

What's next. The authors want future registry studies to include genetic data, to see how genes shape the number of steps. The bigger goal is figuring out what the steps actually are. As always, this is educational only, so talk with your care team about your own situation.

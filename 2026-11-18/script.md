# Script: How might ancient viral RNA in our own DNA start TDP-43 clumping?

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a preprint posted on November 17th, 2026, on bioRxiv. It has not been peer reviewed, so treat it as early science. It comes from Yung-Heng Chang and Josh Dubnau at Stony Brook University, and the title is: "Human Endogenous Retrovirus K RNA triggers cytoplasmic TDP-43 aggregation by initiating pathological nuclear condensates."

The question. In almost everyone with ALS, a protein called TDP-43 leaves the nucleus, where it does its job, and clumps up outside it. Nobody knows what starts that. Here is one clue. Part of our own DNA is made of ancient virus sequences left behind over millions of years. One of them is called HERV-K. TDP-43 normally helps keep these sequences switched off, and when TDP-43 fails, they wake up. This team asked the reverse. If HERV-K wakes up, can that by itself start TDP-43 pathology?

What they did. This is laboratory work in human cell lines, not in people. They used cells whose own TDP-43 carries a fluorescent tag, so they could watch it live, and switched HERV-K on in two ways: by adding it directly, and with a drug that unlocks the cell's own silenced copies. Then they followed TDP-43 over three to five days, and checked whether the cells began mis-cutting two genes, stathmin-2 and UNC13A, the usual fingerprint of TDP-43 losing its day job.

What they found. Turning HERV-K on produced clumps of TDP-43 outside the nucleus, and the mis-cutting of those two genes rose over time. The surprise was where it started. Before those clumps appeared, round structures formed inside the nucleus, coated with the damaged, phosphorylated form of TDP-43. In live movies, some of these nuclear spheres crossed out of the nucleus and became large clumps outside. That flips the usual picture: the trouble may begin inside the nucleus. They also narrowed down the trigger. None of the proteins the virus sequence makes were enough on their own. It was the viral RNA itself, and in particular a short repeating stretch that TDP-43 is known to stick to. Delete that stretch, and the effect largely disappeared.

What it means. For people living with ALS, this is a mechanism idea, not a treatment and not a test. It suggests that ancient viral RNA in our own genome can act as a seed that gathers TDP-43 and starts it aggregating, which would help explain a vicious cycle other groups have described. It is also timely, because antiretroviral drugs are already being tested in ALS trials, and this hints that blocking the viral RNA itself may matter.

Honest caveats. These are immortalized cell lines, with HERV-K pushed to high levels, not neurons from people with ALS. The authors say plainly that the next step is to look for these nuclear spheres in human brain tissue and in animals. And again, it has not been peer reviewed.

What's next. Confirmation in brain tissue and in animals, and testing whether lowering this viral RNA protects neurons. As always, this is educational only, so talk with your care team.

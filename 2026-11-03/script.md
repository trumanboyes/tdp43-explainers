# Script: Seven molecular subtypes in the ALS motor cortex — a map of how ALS varies

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a preprint, posted to bioRxiv on October 30, 2026, so it has not yet been peer reviewed. It comes from Aadya Goel, a student researcher in MIT's PRIMES program, working with Shaojun Pei and Gil Alterovitz at Brigham and Women's Hospital and Harvard Medical School. The title is: "Multiscale Transcriptomic Architecture of the ALS Motor Cortex."

Some background. ALS varies a lot from person to person, which makes treatments hard to test. Researchers have tried to sort ALS into molecular subtypes, based on which genes are switched on in brain tissue. But many earlier studies pooled tissue from different brain and spinal cord regions, which can blur the picture. This team asked: if we look at just one key region, the motor cortex, how is ALS variation organized?

What did they do? They analyzed gene activity in about six hundred motor cortex samples, from three hundred forty-eight donors in the Target ALS brain bank. They scored whole biological pathways, like inflammation, energy use, or nerve signaling, and let a computer group samples with similar patterns.

What they found. Seven reproducible subtypes emerged. When some donors were held out and then assigned to a group, the method put them in the right group more than nine times out of ten. The subtypes ranged from inflammation and tissue damage, to RNA and protein production, to mitochondria and energy metabolism, to nerve-cell signaling. They were not completely separate boxes: they sat on a continuous landscape, with one broad family leaning toward inflammation and damage, and another toward neuronal and metabolic programs. Differences in cell makeup explained part, but not all, of the pattern. Interestingly, the subtypes did not line up with how long people lived with ALS. Instead, they lined up more with genetic background. People with a family history of ALS or frontotemporal dementia, or with a C9orf72 expansion, leaned toward the neuronal and metabolic side, while people without a family history, which describes most people with ALS, leaned toward the inflammation and damage side. The team also flagged six candidate genes for further study.

What it means. For people with sporadic ALS, this adds to evidence that ALS may be several overlapping biological states, not one disease, and that inflammation and tissue-damage programs may be more prominent in people without a family history. Over time, maps like this could help match treatments to the right people.

Honest caveats. This is a preprint, and purely a computer analysis. Brain tissue after death shows late-stage disease at one moment, so it can't show how subtypes change over time or cause anything. The results have not been tested in an independent group of people, and nothing here can be measured in a living person yet. This summary is based on the full preprint.

What's next? The authors call for validation in new cohorts, and for single-cell and spatial studies. This is educational only, not medical advice. Talk with your care team about your own situation.

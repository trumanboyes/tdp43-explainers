# Script: TDP-43 rides several molecular "delivery trucks" down nerve fibers

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on October 9, 2026, in Acta Neuropathologica. It comes from Gorazd Stokin's team, working across the Czech Republic, Cedars-Sinai in Los Angeles, and the U.K., with first author Monica Feole. The title is: "Multiple motor proteins regulate TDP-43 anterograde axonal transport."

Here's the question. In about ninety-seven percent of ALS, including sporadic, gene-negative ALS, a protein called TDP-43 leaves the nucleus and clumps up. But TDP-43 also has a normal job far from the nucleus. It carries messenger RNAs, the cell's recipe cards, down the long nerve fiber, the axon, so proteins can be made right where they're needed. In ALS, axons are among the first things to fail. So how does healthy TDP-43 travel down an axon, and what carries it?

What did they do? They grew human neurons from a stem cell line for forty days. They gave TDP-43 a glowing green tag and filmed it moving inside axons, comparing it with three well-known cargoes. Then they used protein pull-down tests, and a test that lights up only when two proteins sit extremely close together.

What they found. TDP-43 particles were genuinely on the move, traveling in both directions in roughly equal amounts. Heading outward, toward the nerve endings, they moved fast and steadily, at speeds almost identical to a classic fast cargo called A-P-P. TDP-43 latched onto the outbound machinery more strongly than the return machinery. Outbound, it linked up with an adaptor called K-L-C-1, and through it with all three kinesin-1 motors: K-I-F-5A, B, and C. Lowering K-L-C-1 sharply reduced that signal, a check that the link is real. TDP-43 also engaged a second, separate motor, K-I-F-1A, which normally rushes synapse parts to nerve endings. For the return trip, it connected with the dynein motor complex.

Why does this matter for sporadic ALS? Having several motors may be a built-in backup system. The authors suggest that when TDP-43 builds up in the wrong place in ALS, several of these delivery routes could be disrupted at once, starving distant parts of the nerve of the recipes they need. Notably, faults in K-I-F-5A and K-I-F-1A themselves are already linked to some cases of ALS. These transport routes could become future drug targets.

Honest caveats. This is basic biology in healthy human neurons in a dish, not ALS cells and not patients. The neurons came from one stem cell line, and weren't motor neurons from people with ALS. The glowing TDP-43 was extra, added on top of the cell's own. Each cargo was filmed separately, so the team didn't show them traveling together. And no treatment was tested.

What's next? Repeating these measurements in motor neurons made from people with ALS, including sporadic ALS, to see which routes break first, and whether protecting them keeps axons healthy. That's tonight's paper. This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

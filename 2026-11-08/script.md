# Script: How faulty TDP-43 jams a neuron's protein shredder

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published in the Journal of Biomedical Science on November 4, 2026, by Pitchapa Bubphachat, Chi-Chen Sarah Huang, and colleagues at Taipei Medical University and partner institutions. The title is: "The TNKS/PI31–proteasome axis as a central node in TDP-43 neuropathies."

The question. Cells clear out worn-out and misfolded proteins using a machine called the proteasome, a kind of molecular shredder. In most people with ALS, the protein TDP-43 builds up in clumps, along with other proteins tagged for disposal. That suggests the shredder is not keeping up. Motor neurons have very long axons, so proteasomes must also be carried along those axons to where they are needed. The team asked: how does faulty TDP-43 interfere with the proteasome, both its activity and its delivery?

What they did. They worked in mouse nerve cell lines, in neurons grown from mice, and in a mouse model that carries an ALS-linked TDP-43 mutation called A315T. They measured proteasome activity, filmed proteasomes moving along living axons, and tracked two helper proteins: an enzyme called tankyrase, or T-N-K-S, and a proteasome partner called P-I 31. Normally, tankyrase adds a small chemical tag to P-I 31. That tag helps the proteasome assemble into its fully active form, and helps it move along the axon.

What they found. Faulty TDP-43, even as small clusters called oligomers rather than large clumps, lowered proteasome activity and led to a build-up of proteins tagged for disposal. In motor neurons from the ALS model mice, fewer proteasomes were on the move, about 36 percent versus 57 percent in normal neurons, and they tended to move more slowly. Proteasomes, P-I 31, and TDP-43 piled up near the cell body. The key mechanism: faulty TDP-43 grabbed tankyrase and pulled it into insoluble clumps, so less was free to tag P-I 31. When the researchers blocked tankyrase with a drug, normal neurons showed the same transport and proteasome problems. And a TDP-43 version that could not bind tankyrase still clumped, but harmed proteasome activity less. So clumping alone was not the whole story; the grab on tankyrase mattered.

What it means. This adds detail to how TDP-43 problems could weaken a neuron's cleanup system. It points to a more targeted idea than simply boosting all proteasomes: protecting the tankyrase and P-I 31 link.

Honest caveats. This is lab work in cells and mice, mostly with TDP-43 made at higher than normal levels, and one mutation that is rare in people. There was no human tissue and no treatment was tested. It is early science, not a therapy.

What's next. The authors plan to test this pathway in motor neurons made from patients' own stem cells, and to design molecules that block the TDP-43 grab on tankyrase. This is educational only, not medical advice. Talk with your care team about your own situation.

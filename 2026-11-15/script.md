# Script: Blood IGF-1 and short-term change in ALS: a small, early clue

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a preprint posted to medRxiv on November 6th, 2026, by Chai Lim, Julie Atkin, and colleagues at the Motor Neuron Disease Research Centre at Macquarie University in Sydney, Australia. It has not yet been peer reviewed. The title is: "Circulating insulin-like growth factor 1 and short-term functional change in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis."

The question. ALS progresses at very different speeds in different people, and nobody can yet predict who will decline slowly or quickly. IGF-1 is a growth hormone-like signal in the blood that helps keep muscles and nerve cells healthy. The team asked: do people with higher IGF-1 in their blood lose function more slowly over the following months?

What they did. They used blood samples and clinic records from a research biobank: 37 people with ALS and 25 people without it, of similar age and sex. They measured IGF-1 in stored blood, and tracked the ALS functional rating score across clinic visits, usually about six months apart. Then they checked whether the IGF-1 level at one visit predicted how much the score changed by the next visit, accounting for age, sex, body weight, and time between visits.

What they found. First, average IGF-1 was not clearly different between people with and without ALS, and it did not differ between people grouped as slow, average, or fast progressors. But looking visit to visit, in 23 people with enough complete data, higher IGF-1 went along with less decline at the next visit. For each step up in IGF-1 about the size of the usual spread between people, they lost about 1.4 fewer points on the 48-point scale by the next visit. That result was just barely statistically significant, and with a stricter test suited to small studies, it was no longer significant. It did stay in the same direction when each person was left out in turn.

What it means. This hints that IGF-1 might be one sign of the body's overall reserve, a kind of metabolic resilience, in ALS. But it is an observation, not proof of cause. It does not mean that taking IGF-1 or a supplement will slow ALS. In fact, earlier trials that gave people IGF-1 as a drug did not show clear benefit, and the authors say their results do not change that. The paper did not report genetic testing, but most people with ALS have the sporadic form, so this clinic group likely was mostly sporadic.

To be honest about the limits: this is a small, single-center study that has not been peer reviewed, the key result is borderline, and it has not been checked in a separate group of patients.

What's next. The team calls for larger studies that follow people over time, with more measures of muscle, nutrition, and how the IGF-1 pathway is actually working. Until then, this is an interesting clue, not a test or treatment. As always, this is educational only, so talk with your care team before changing anything.

# Script: Can a blood protein show how far ALS has progressed?

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a preprint on medRxiv, posted on November 20th, 2026. That means it has not yet been peer reviewed. It comes from a team led by Johnathan Cooper-Knock and Pamela Shaw at the University of Sheffield in the UK, working with researchers in Oxford, Israel and the United States. The title is: "Circulating gelsolin tracks disease stage in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis across four cohorts."

The question. A blood test for neurofilament light, or NfL, is the best-known blood marker in ALS. It reflects how fast nerve fibers are being damaged. But people give blood at different points in very different disease courses. So the authors asked: could some blood proteins tell us how far someone's illness has gone, rather than how fast it is moving?

What they did. They measured more than five thousand blood proteins, plus thousands of small molecules in blood and spinal fluid, from 94 people with ALS and controls in Sheffield. For people who had died, they defined "stage" as the fraction of the time from first symptom to death that had passed when the blood was drawn. They checked the results in three more groups, from Tel Aviv, Oxford and the Answer ALS program.

What they found. Sixty-eight proteins tracked stage across the four groups. They pointed to body-wide inflammation, loss of muscle, and a shift toward an older molecular profile. The most consistent was a protein called gelsolin, which fell as the disease advanced, in all four groups. It also dropped over time within the same people. Gelsolin tracked how far the disease had gone, but not how fast it was moving. NfL showed the opposite pattern. Used together, the two markers predicted survival better than either one alone.

What it means. One blood sample might one day tell two separate things: how fast ALS is moving, from NfL, and how far along it is, from gelsolin. That could help balance groups in clinical trials, and help make sense of other blood markers. For sporadic ALS, the most common form, it adds a possible new tool for research.

Honest caveats. This is a preprint, so it has not been checked by peer review. Stage can only be calculated after someone has died, so it is partly built from survival itself. Gelsolin explained only part of the differences between people. The 68-protein list was not corrected for multiple testing, and the metabolite results come from one center. This is not a test anyone can order today.

What's next. The authors suggest testing gelsolin with simpler single-protein blood tests, alongside NfL, and using stage to plan and read trials. As always, this is an educational summary, not medical advice.

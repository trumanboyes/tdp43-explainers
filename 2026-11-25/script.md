# Script: Can a designed pill break up TDP-43 clumps? ACE-339 in cells and mice

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a preprint on bioRxiv, posted on November 17th, 2026, so it has not yet been peer reviewed. It comes from Marcela Kokes, Vidhu Mathur, and colleagues at the biotech company Acelot in South San Francisco, working with teams at Macquarie University in Sydney, the University of Texas Medical Branch, and UC Santa Barbara. The title is: "In silico designed small molecule targets misfolded TDP-43 to rescue function and ALS pathology."

The question. In almost all people with ALS, a protein called TDP-43 leaves its normal home in the cell's nucleus and clumps together in the surrounding cytoplasm. That causes two problems at once: the clumps themselves are harmful, and the nucleus loses the TDP-43 it needs to splice genes correctly. So the team asked: can a small, swallowable drug break up those TDP-43 clumps, and would that also let TDP-43 go back to its job?

What they did. The company used its machine-learning drug design platform, called RESOLUTE, to design and screen candidate molecules. The lead compound, ACE-339, was then tested on purified TDP-43, on TDP-43 clumps grown from the brains of people who died with ALS, in stressed human cells and human stem-cell-derived motor neurons, and in two mouse models that make large amounts of harmful human TDP-43. They also ran computer simulations of how the drug might grip the clumps.

What they found. ACE-339 broke up TDP-43 clumps in the test tube, and left normally folded proteins alone. In stressed cells, it reduced TDP-43 clumps and helped TDP-43 return to the nucleus. It also cut back faulty splicing in genes like STMN2 and UNC13A, which go wrong when TDP-43 is lost. Given by mouth, the drug reached the brain. In one mouse model it lowered harmful TDP-43 and brain inflammation, and improved a motor test. In a second, more severe model, the higher dose extended median survival by about eleven and a half days, roughly 28 percent, and lowered neurofilament light, a blood marker of nerve damage.

What it means. This is an early but interesting step toward a pill that targets TDP-43 directly, the protein problem found in most cases of ALS. The authors say compounds like this are being developed further toward clinical trials.

Honest caveats. This is a preprint written by the company that owns the drug. All of the evidence comes from test tubes, cells, and mice, often at high doses, and some measures in mice did not reach statistical significance. Mouse survival gains often do not carry over to people.

What's next. The drug still needs safety studies, and then trials in people. As always, this is educational only, and not medical advice.

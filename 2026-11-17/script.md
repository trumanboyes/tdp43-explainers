# Script: Do ALS blood immune cells differ between men and women?

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published online on November 4th, 2026, in the Journal of Clinical Neurology. It is peer reviewed and free to read. It comes from Seung Hyun Kim's team at Hanyang University in Seoul, working with Gerald Pfeffer, Minh Dang Nguyen, and colleagues at the University of Calgary. The title is: "A Male-Specific Immunometabolic Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cell Transcriptomic Signature in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis."

The question. ALS is not the same in men and women. Men get it more often, tend to get it earlier, and are more likely to burn energy faster than expected. Nobody really knows why. One idea is the immune system, which also differs between the sexes. So this team asked: in people with ALS, do the immune cells in the blood look different in men than in women?

What they did. They studied 30 people with sporadic ALS, 15 men and 15 women, plus 30 healthy volunteers, spouses of participants, matched by sex. Everyone with ALS had been screened, and no one had a known ALS gene variant or a family history. The team took a standard blood sample, isolated a group of white blood cells called P-B-M-Cs, and measured which genes were switched on or off. They checked key results with a second lab test, and compared them with neurofilament light, a blood marker of nerve damage.

What they found. The difference between the sexes was striking. In men with ALS, 3,674 genes differed from healthy men. In women with ALS, only 8 genes differed from healthy women. In men, genes for the mitochondria, the cell's power plants, were turned down, while genes for collagen, tissue remodeling, and inflammation were turned up. A second test confirmed these changes. But this male pattern did not track with neurofilament levels, with how fast the disease was moving, or with how severe it was.

What it means. This suggests that ALS may affect the body's immune and energy systems differently in men and women, which could help explain some of the sex differences doctors see. It is a clue about biology, not a test or a treatment, and it does not change care today. It also reminds researchers to look at men and women separately in studies.

Honest caveats. This is a small study, 60 people from one hospital in Korea, measured at a single point in time after symptoms began. So it cannot tell whether these changes come before ALS or follow it. There was no comparison group with other brain diseases, and the women had been living with ALS a bit longer on average when sampled.

What's next. The authors call for larger and more diverse groups, repeated blood samples over time, including before symptoms, and tools that look at single cell types. As always, this is educational only, so talk with your care team about your own situation.

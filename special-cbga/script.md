# Script: CBGA (cannabigerolic acid) and ALS — special episode

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn – Warm Grounded Narrator). Plain-English explainer for people with ALS and their families, not medical advice. Oct 28, 2026.*

Welcome to this special episode for people living with ALS and their families. Today's topic is CBGA, short for cannabigerolic acid, and what the research does and does not say about it in ALS and other brain diseases.

First, what it is. CBGA is sometimes called the mother cannabinoid. Inside the cannabis plant, enzymes turn CBGA into the acid forms of THC, CBD, and CBC. When CBGA is heated or aged, it loses a piece of its structure and becomes CBG, cannabigerol. Neither CBGA nor CBG is intoxicating.

The most important point up front: almost everything we know comes from cells in dishes and from animals. No clinical trial of CBGA in people has been published, for any condition, and there is no known safe or effective human dose.

What have lab studies found? In motor-neuron-like cells exposed to inflammatory signals, CBG reduced inflammation markers and oxidative stress and helped cells survive. In mice with a Huntington's-like brain injury, CBG protected neurons, improved movement, and calmed microglia, the brain's immune cells. In a genetic Huntington's mouse, the benefit was much smaller. Note that most of this work used CBG, not CBGA itself.

CBGA has been studied directly in seizures. In 2021, Australian researchers tested it in a mouse model of Dravet syndrome, a severe childhood epilepsy. CBGA reduced heat-triggered seizures and boosted a standard seizure drug. But it made seizures worse in another test, and a high dose increased spontaneous seizures. So more is not necessarily better, and effects can cut both ways.

What about ALS specifically? We found no published study of CBGA in any ALS model, and no study in people with ALS. A related, lab-made CBG derivative helped ALS mice in one 2018 study, but that compound is not CBGA and is not sold as a supplement. Reviews in 2025 and 2026 call acidic cannabinoids promising but underexplored.

Safety and interactions. In lab tests, most cannabinoids studied blocked liver enzymes that process many common medicines, especially one called CYP2C9. Whether CBGA does this in people is unknown, so drug interactions are possible and untested.

Product quality is another concern. CBGA and CBG products are largely unregulated. In a well-known study of CBD products bought online, only about three in ten were labeled accurately, and some contained THC that was not listed. If you look at any product, ask for a recent independent lab certificate.

Bottom line: CBGA is an interesting molecule, but there is no evidence that it helps people with ALS, no known dose, and real unknowns about safety. Please talk with your ALS care team before trying it or any supplement.

This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

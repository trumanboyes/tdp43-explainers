# Script: Why ALS genes and ALS tissue tell different mitochondria stories

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a new preprint, posted to bioRxiv on November 11th, 2026, by Aminah Ali, Alfredo Iacoangeli, Ammar Al-Chalabi, and colleagues at King's College London. The title is: "Investigating the causal and consequential role of the nuclear encoded mitochondrial genome in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis."

The question. Mitochondria are the power plants inside our cells, and they have long been suspected in ALS. Most of their parts, over a thousand proteins, are built from genes in the cell's main DNA, not the mitochondria's own small genome. The team asked a chicken-and-egg question: do inherited genetic differences in these mitochondrial genes help cause ALS, or are the mitochondrial changes seen in ALS tissue mostly a response to the disease?

What they did. First, they used the largest European genetic study of ALS so far: about twenty-seven thousand people with ALS and a hundred and ten thousand without. Most people in studies like this have no family history of the disease. They checked whether ALS risk clusters in particular mitochondrial pathways. Then they looked at gene activity in more than fifteen hundred samples: brain and spinal cord donated after death, and motor neurons grown from stem cells.

What they found. Inherited ALS risk was modestly concentrated in one area: the machinery mitochondria use to build their own proteins, especially the mitoribosome, their protein factory. But the direction was mixed. For some genes, lower activity went with higher risk; for others, higher activity did. In ALS motor cortex, mitochondrial genes were broadly more active than in controls. Yet these tissue changes did not line up with the genetic predictions any better than chance. Groupings by mitochondrial activity did not clearly predict survival, and the communication between the cell's main DNA and the mitochondria's own DNA looked different in ALS tissue.

What it means. Genes and tissue both point toward mitochondrial protein building, but they may be telling two different stories. Much of what we see in tissue from late-stage ALS may be the body responding to disease, rather than what started it. For sporadic ALS, the authors' practical point is about treatments: picking a mitochondrial target that is only a downstream response could be ineffective, or even counterproductive.

Honest caveats. This is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed. The genetic signal is modest. The genetic data come only from people of European ancestry, the tissue was mostly from late-stage disease, and bulk samples mix many cell types together.

What's next. Studies of individual cell types, and of cells sampled earlier in disease, are needed to separate cause from consequence. This is educational only, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

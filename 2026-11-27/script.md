# Script: Can a neuron's 'cell-cycle' signal predict how fast ALS progresses?

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a peer-reviewed study in the journal Brain, published online on November 11th, 2026. It comes from Jonathan Plessis-Belair and Roger Sher at Stony Brook University in New York. The title is: "Neuronal cell-cycle re-entry stratifies clinical trajectories in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis."

The question. Adult neurons are not supposed to divide. But in ALS and other brain diseases, some neurons switch back on the machinery cells use to copy themselves, called the cell cycle. ALS also varies hugely from person to person: some people progress quickly, others slowly. The authors asked two things. Does the cell-cycle signal line up with how fast ALS progresses? And what switches it on in motor neurons?

What they did. First, they used Answer ALS, a large public research program that turned patients' own cells into stem cells and then into motor neurons. They looked at gene activity in cells from 624 people, including 445 with ALS, and linked it to clinical records. Then, in lab-grown human motor neurons, they used a drug called importazole to jam the door that carries proteins into the cell's nucleus. That door is known to fail in ALS.

What they found. One group of patients had cells with the strongest cell-cycle signal, and that group showed the slowest decline on the ALS functional rating scale. People with only moderate activation tended to do worse. Survival did not differ between groups. In the dish, jamming the nuclear door caused TDP-43, the protein that clumps in most ALS, to leave the nucleus and form clumps. It also set off a short wave of cell-cycle activity. Neurons that went on to start copying their DNA built up DNA damage and died. Blocking that step kept more neurons alive. So did very low doses of two breast cancer drugs, palbociclib and abemaciclib, which block proteins called CDK4 and 6. Only abemaciclib reached statistical significance by day seven.

What it means. The study suggests that how far a motor neuron goes into the cell cycle may matter, and that starting to copy DNA could be a point of no return. It also hints that patients' own cells carry clues about how their disease may progress.

Honest caveats. The patient findings are correlations from stem-cell-grown neurons, not brain tissue. The drug experiments were short and done only in dishes. These cancer drugs have not been tested in people with ALS, and no one should take them for ALS outside a trial.

What's next. The authors call for longer studies in genetic and sporadic ALS models, and testing whether cell-cycle signatures could help group patients in trials. As always, this is educational only, and not medical advice.

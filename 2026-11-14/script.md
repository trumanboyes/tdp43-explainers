# Script: A small cannabis extract trial in ALS: no clear effect on progression

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published in BMJ Neurology Open on November 11th, 2026, by Berzenn Urbi, Simon Broadley, and colleagues at Griffith University and Gold Coast Hospital in Australia, with Duke University in the United States. The title is: "A phase two, randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial of cannabis extract for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis: the EMERALD trial."

The question. Many people with ALS say they use medicinal cannabis, mostly for pain, stiffness, sleep, or appetite. Animal studies hinted that cannabis compounds might protect nerve cells. So the team asked: in a proper trial against a placebo, does a cannabis extract slow ALS?

What they did. People with ALS at one Australian hospital were randomly assigned to a cannabis oil or a look-alike placebo made from hemp seed oil, for six months. Neither they nor the doctors knew who got which. The oil mostly contained CBD, with a small amount of THC. Each person slowly raised the dose over two weeks, up to what they could tolerate. The main measure was the ALS functional rating score after six months.

What they found. Recruiting was very hard. The plan called for 38 people, but only 17 enrolled, and just 8 finished the full six months, 4 in each group. Reasons included COVID, a rule that people had to stop driving, and low-dose CBD becoming available over the counter. At six months, there was no clear difference in the functional score: about 32 with cannabis versus about 34 with placebo, a gap that could easily be chance. Breathing, stiffness, pain, weight, and quality of life also showed no clear differences. Side effects were similar overall. In extra analyses done after the fact, the cannabis group reported less usual pain and scored better on one thinking test, but these were tiny numbers.

What it means. This trial does not show that cannabis slows ALS. The authors say so plainly. Most participants had the sporadic form of ALS, so this applies to the common type. The extract seemed reasonably well tolerated, and the hints about pain and thinking are ideas to test, not proof. If you are curious about cannabis for symptoms, it is worth talking it through with your care team, including possible interactions with other medicines.

Honest caveats. With only 8 people finishing, the study was far too small to detect a real effect, or to rule one out. Nearly half did not complete the six months, often because their illness progressed, which can skew results. The pain and thinking findings came from analyses added later, and could easily be chance.

What's next. The authors call for larger trials across several centers, with a closer look at pain and thinking. This is educational only, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

# Script: When TDP-43 leaves the nucleus (iPSC motor neurons)

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on October 10, 2026, in the open-access journal Communications Biology. The first author is Tze Khee Chan, with senior author Aaron Zefrin Fernandis, from a Merck and National University of Singapore collaboration. The title is: "Human iPSC-derived motor neurons with TDP-43 nuclear loss present early ALS pathological phenotypes."

In plain English: they built a lab model of what happens in nearly all ALS — when TDP-43, the cell's RNA editor, gets locked out of the nucleus.

They used human stem cells turned into spinal motor neurons, and gave them a mutation that keeps TDP-43 out of the control room. Those neurons looked a lot like early ALS. Their branches grew poorly. They struggled to regrow after injury. And they fired too easily — a kind of hyperexcitability patients' motor neurons often show.

Looking under the hood with proteomics and metabolomics, the team saw early stress signals: altered oxidative stress and changes in how chemical messengers are released. The classic TDP-43 fingerprint was there too: mis-splicing of STMN2, which helps axons grow and repair, and of KCNQ2, a potassium channel that helps calm electrical firing.

The hopeful part: a splice-switching antisense oligonucleotide — a short drug-like strand of genetic material — restored normal STMN2 splicing and function in these motor neurons. So fixing that one broken instruction helped the cells recover.

Why this matters for sporadic ALS: most people with ALS, including those without a known gene mutation, share this TDP-43 nuclear-loss problem. A model that captures early changes and responds to an STMN2-targeting ASO is useful for testing RNA therapies before they reach people.

Caveats, honestly. This is cells in a dish, not patients. The cells carry an engineered localization mutation, not the exact biology of every person with sporadic ALS. Merck funded the work, and several authors are company employees. Benefit in people still has to be proven in trials.

Still, the takeaway is clear: locking TDP-43 out of the nucleus is enough to kick off early ALS-like damage in human motor neurons — and directly correcting a splicing defect can rescue them in the lab. That's tonight's paper. This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

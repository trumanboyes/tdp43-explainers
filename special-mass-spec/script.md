# Script: How to read a mass spectrometry (LC-MS) report — special episode

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn – Warm Grounded Narrator). Plain-English explainer for a general audience, not medical advice. Oct 28, 2026.*

Welcome to this special episode. Today we'll walk through how to read a mass spectrometry report, often labeled LC-MS, in plain English. This is a general guide for anyone curious, including people living with ALS and their families.

First, what LC-MS does. It's two tools joined together. Liquid chromatography, the LC, pushes a dissolved sample through a packed column, which separates the mixture so different compounds come out at different times. Then the mass spectrometer, the MS, gives those molecules an electric charge and measures their mass-to-charge ratio, written m over z.

The first picture in most reports is the chromatogram. Time runs along the bottom; this is called retention time, usually in minutes. Signal strength, or intensity, runs up the side. Each peak is something coming off the column. The area under a peak roughly tracks how much is there, but different compounds respond differently, so a real amount can only be calculated against a reference standard of known concentration.

Click on a peak and you get a mass spectrum. Now the bottom axis is m over z. Because most ions carry a single charge, m over z is usually just the mass. But a molecule rarely shows up bare. In positive mode it often picks up a proton, written M plus H, which reads one unit heavier than the molecule. It may grab sodium instead, M plus Na, plus twenty-three, or potassium, M plus K, plus thirty-nine. In negative mode it more often loses a proton, M minus H, one unit lighter.

You'll also see smaller extra peaks. Fragments appear when a molecule breaks apart in the instrument; a loss of eighteen usually means it shed a water molecule. Isotope peaks sit one or two units above the main peak, because a small share of carbon atoms are the heavier carbon-thirteen.

Here's a worked example using caffeine, a familiar compound with a mass of about one ninety-four. In positive mode you'd expect a main peak at about one ninety-five, which is M plus H, and perhaps two seventeen, M plus Na. Just above one ninety-five sits a small isotope peak at one ninety-six. And if the instrument breaks the molecule apart, a well-known fragment appears near one thirty-eight, after caffeine loses a piece weighing fifty-seven. Several peaks, one compound.

Now the limits. A single run cannot prove that something is absent. A compound may not ionize well in the chosen mode, may be below the detection limit, or may fall outside the mass range that was scanned. And a match in mass alone is a clue, not an identity; confirmation needs a reference standard run under the same conditions, matching both retention time and spectrum.

So check whether a result is qualitative, meaning detected or not, or quantitative, meaning a measured amount with units. Good questions for the lab are: which mode and mass range did you use? What are your detection and quantitation limits? Did you run reference standards? And how confident is each identification?

The bottom line: retention time tells you when, m over z tells you what mass, and peak area hints at how much, with standards. Before acting on any result, talk with a pharmacist or your care team.

This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

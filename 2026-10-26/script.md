# Script: Can one gene help sort sporadic ALS into biological groups?

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a preprint, posted on bioRxiv on October 8, 2026, so it has not been peer reviewed yet. It comes from Cedars-Sinai Medical Center in Los Angeles, with first author Priyanka Bhandary and senior author Zhiping Paul Wang. The title is: "CCT2 expression marks a translation-activated state in sporadic ALS motor neurons and is associated with functional decline."

Some background. About nine out of ten people with ALS have the sporadic form, with no single gene to explain it. Many researchers think sporadic ALS is really several different biological diseases grouped under one name. If that's true, a drug that helps one group could look like a failure when it's tested on everyone at once.

What did they do? They used data from Answer ALS, a large project that turned blood cells from volunteers into motor neurons grown in the lab. They looked at motor neurons from 391 people with sporadic ALS, plus people with known ALS genes and healthy volunteers. They measured which genes were switched on, which proteins were present, and each person's full DNA sequence.

What they found. First, even the two best-known genetic forms, C9orf72 and SOD1, did not look alike in these cells. Then, within sporadic ALS, patients fell into three groups, based on energy metabolism, cell-growth programs, and inflammation. About half were in a quiet group, low on all three. About a fifth were high on everything, though that group was partly explained by differences in how well the cells grew. And about a quarter were in a third group, which was high on metabolism and growth signals.

That third group could be picked out by a single gene, called CCT2, with good accuracy. The signal showed up at the protein level too. And its pattern linked to growth signals called MYC and mTOR. Importantly, nobody's DNA explained which group they were in. The authors think these are states the cells are in, not inherited categories.

What it means. If this holds up, a simple CCT2 measurement might someday help sort people with sporadic ALS into biological groups for clinical trials, so treatments are tested in the people most likely to benefit. But it's early. Higher CCT2 was linked to faster decline on the ALS functional rating scale, but that link weakened and was no longer significant after accounting for how well the cells grew. And there was not enough data to test whether it relates to survival.

Honest caveats. This is a preprint. The cells came from one lab system, with variable quality. There's no independent group of patient cells to confirm it yet. And everything about mechanism is inferred, not tested.

What's next? The authors want to repeat this in a separate set of patient cells, check survival, and run experiments on what these growth signals do to motor neurons. This is educational only, not medical advice. Talk with your care team about your own situation.

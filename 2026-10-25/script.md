# Script: Why an Alzheimer's blood test reads high in ALS, and how to fix it

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on October 22, 2026, in the journal Nature Communications. It comes from a group of European centers led by Martin Luther University Halle-Wittenberg in Germany, with first author Samir Abu-Rumeileh and senior author Markus Otto. The title is: "Tau isoform mapping across tissues and biofluids reveals a signature distinguishing Alzheimer's disease from amyotrophic lateral sclerosis."

First, some background. A blood test called p-tau217 has become one of the best ways to detect Alzheimer's disease. But people with ALS often have high p-tau217 in their blood too, even when their spinal fluid looks normal. Tau comes in different forms. The brain mostly makes a small form. Muscles and nerves outside the brain make a larger form called "big tau." The question was: in ALS, is that blood signal coming from the brain, or from muscle?

What did they do? They studied blood from 139 people with ALS, 101 people with Alzheimer's disease or early memory problems, and 75 people with other conditions, from centers in Germany and Italy. They first used standard commercial tests. Then they added an extra step that pulls out only the brain form of tau before measuring. They also examined tau in donated brain, spinal cord, and muscle tissue.

What they found. With the standard tests, tau markers were raised in both Alzheimer's and ALS. In spinal fluid, the brain form of tau was not raised in ALS, except in a small number of people who also had Alzheimer's-type changes. After the extra brain-only step, the blood signal stayed high in Alzheimer's but was no longer raised in ALS. That made it much easier to tell the two diseases apart. In muscle, the only p-tau217 they found was on big tau. And the links between standard tau results and ALS severity disappeared after the brain-only step, suggesting those links came from muscle, not brain.

What does it mean for people with sporadic ALS? If you have ALS and a blood test shows high p-tau217, that does not necessarily mean you have Alzheimer's disease. It may simply reflect tau released from muscle that has lost its nerve supply. A brain-specific test could help find the people with ALS who truly have Alzheimer's-type changes. Someday, a muscle-tau test might even help track nerve loss, but that still has to be built.

Honest caveats. This was a look back at stored samples. The brain-only test was done in a smaller group of 50 people with ALS. Tissue samples were few and not from the same people. The extra step is complex and not yet available in routine care. And two authors work for the company that makes the antibody used.

What's next? Larger studies, a standardized brain-only test, and tests for muscle tau. That's tonight's paper. This is educational, not medical advice. Please talk with your care team about your own tests. Thanks for listening.

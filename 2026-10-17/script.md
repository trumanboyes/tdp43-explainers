# Script: A sharper test for HDGFL2-CE, a cryptic protein made when TDP-43 fails

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on October 9, 2026, in Cell Reports Medicine. It comes from Leonard Petrucelli's group at the University of Miami and Mayo Clinic, with first authors Kathryn Citrano and Vidhya Jawahar. The title is: "Anti-H-D-G-F-L-2 cryptic protein antibodies provide a sensitive readout of TDP-43 pathology and clinical disease features."

Here's the question. In most people with ALS, including sporadic, gene-negative ALS, a protein called TDP-43 leaves the cell nucleus and clumps up elsewhere in the nerve cell. TDP-43 normally edits RNA messages. When it's missing, cells keep bits of RNA that should be cut out, called cryptic exons. Some get translated into abnormal proteins, like a cryptic version of a protein called H-D-G-F-L-2. Earlier studies found it in brain, spinal fluid, and blood from people with TDP-43 diseases. But the existing antibodies weren't sensitive enough to study it well.

What did they do? They made new antibodies that recognize the abnormal cryptic piece, built sensitive lab tests with them, and checked them using lab-made proteins, human cell models, and donated brain tissue. Then they measured the cryptic protein in frontal cortex from 450 people: 79 healthy controls, 222 with frontotemporal dementia with TDP-43 pathology, 89 with that dementia plus motor neuron disease, and 60 with motor neuron disease alone.

What they found. The new test detected much smaller amounts than the first-generation antibody, and could tell the abnormal protein apart from normal H-D-G-F-L-2. Under the microscope, the cryptic protein built up in neurons that had TDP-43 clumps and had lost TDP-43 from the nucleus. Levels were clearly higher in both dementia groups than in controls, and higher levels went along with more abnormal TDP-43, earlier symptom onset, and shorter survival. In the motor-neuron-disease-only group, the increase was not significant after adjusting for age and sex, likely because the frontal cortex carries less TDP-43 pathology in those patients. The test also reliably detected small amounts added to spinal fluid samples.

Why does this matter for sporadic ALS? Right now there is no reliable way to see TDP-43 dysfunction in a living person. A sensitive marker tied directly to that dysfunction could, if it works in spinal fluid or blood, help researchers track it in living patients.

Honest caveats. This is mostly autopsy brain tissue, mainly from dementia cases. The test has not yet measured naturally occurring levels in patients' spinal fluid or blood. Disease and control groups overlap, so it's unlikely to work alone as a diagnostic. And the links with onset and survival are associations, not cause and effect.

What's next? The team plans ultrasensitive tests for spinal fluid and blood, and antibodies against other cryptic proteins, to build a multi-marker panel. That's tonight's paper. This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

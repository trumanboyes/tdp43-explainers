# Script: Can your body clock hint at how ALS will progress?

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published online on October 16, 2026, in the Annals of Neurology. It comes from the Turin ALS Center at the University of Torino in Italy, with first author Alessandro Bombaci and senior author Alessandro Cicolin. The title is: "Salivary Dim-Light Melatonin Onset is Associated with Functional Decline, Respiratory Symptom Emergence, and Survival in Early Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis."

Some background. Many people with ALS have trouble with sleep. Our sleep is timed by an internal body clock, called the circadian rhythm. One of the best ways to read that clock is melatonin. In the evening, as the room gets dim, the body starts releasing melatonin. The time when it first starts to rise is called dim-light melatonin onset. It's a bit like reading the hands on your body's clock. The question was simple: is this clock shifted in early ALS, and does its timing tell us anything about how the disease will go?

What did they do? They enrolled fifty people with ALS, all within eighteen months of their first symptoms. At home, in dim light, each person gave six saliva samples around their usual bedtime. The team measured melatonin in each sample and worked out when it started to rise. They compared this with healthy people of the same age and sex. They checked everyone's ALS again after six months, and followed survival.

What they found. People with ALS had an earlier melatonin onset than healthy people: about 8:24 in the evening, versus about 8:58. That's roughly half an hour earlier, even though both groups went to bed at similar times. Within the ALS group, timing also mattered. A later melatonin onset went with worse function at the start, and faster decline. It also went with new breathing symptoms by six months, such as shortness of breath or trouble breathing lying flat. People with a later onset had clearly higher odds of developing them. And it went with shorter survival without a tracheostomy. In twenty-eight people tested again after six months, melatonin timing did not change.

What it means for people with ALS. This suggests the body clock is already affected early in ALS. A cheap, at-home saliva test might one day help predict who will need breathing support sooner. But the authors call these findings exploratory. To be clear, this study did not test melatonin as a treatment, so it says nothing about whether taking melatonin helps.

Honest caveats. Only fifty people took part, from one region of Italy. There was no outside group to check the results. And this summary is based on the abstract, since the full paper is behind a paywall.

What's next? Larger, independent studies are needed before this test could be used in clinics. This is educational only, not medical advice. Talk with your care team about your own situation.

# Script: Can a blood test spot ALS years before symptoms?

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published online on October 20, 2026, in the Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle. It comes from West China Hospital at Sichuan University, with first author Wei-Ming Su and senior authors Bei Cao and Yong-Ping Chen. The title is: "Plasma Proteomic Signatures Predict Incident Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis: A Prospective Study Stratified by C9orf72 Expansion."

Some background. By the time ALS symptoms appear, many motor neurons have already been lost. So researchers want to know what changes in the body years earlier. Most blood studies look at people who already have ALS, which can't tell cause from effect.

What did they do? They used the UK Biobank, a large health study in Britain. About fifty-two thousand people without ALS gave blood between 2006 and 2010. The team measured almost three thousand proteins in that blood, then followed everyone for about twelve years. Two hundred ninety-one people went on to develop ALS. Thirty-four of them carried the C9orf72 gene change. The other two hundred fifty-seven did not, so most were likely sporadic cases.

What they found. Nine proteins were linked to later ALS. The strongest by far was neurofilament light, a piece of nerve-cell scaffolding that leaks into blood when nerves are damaged. People with higher levels had nearly three times the risk per step up. Several others were muscle proteins. A few, like ITGAV and CD248, went the other way: higher levels came with lower risk. Combined with age and sex, the proteins predicted who would develop ALS within three to ten years fairly well. In people without the C9orf72 change, neurofilament light seemed to start rising about nine years before diagnosis, and peaked one to two years before. Higher levels also went with a younger age at onset. But none of these early blood proteins predicted survival after diagnosis.

What it means for people with sporadic ALS. This supports the idea that ALS starts quietly, years before symptoms, even when there's no known gene cause. The muscle proteins hint that muscle may be involved early, though the blood can't show where they came from. For anyone already diagnosed, this doesn't change care. Its value is for science: picking people for prevention studies, and timing future treatments earlier.

Honest caveats. There was no outside group to confirm the results. They split the same cohort in half, and only some proteins held up. Each person gave blood once, so the nine-year rise is a model across people, not tracked within anyone. The gene-carrier group was small. Most participants were White. And ALS is rare, so even a "high-risk" result means a small chance. This is not a screening test.

What's next? Confirming these proteins in other cohorts, with repeated blood draws over time. This is educational only, not medical advice. Talk with your care team about your own situation.

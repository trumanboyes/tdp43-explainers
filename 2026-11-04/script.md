# Script: Rate and shape — why one number doesn't capture how ALS progresses

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published in the Annals of Neurology on October 27, 2026. It comes from Andrea Calvo, Adriano Chiò, Jessica Mandrioli and colleagues at the ALS centers in Turin and Modena, Italy. The title is: "Rate and Shape of Functional Decline in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Identify Clinically Distinct ALS Phenotypes."

The question. Doctors and clinical trials usually track ALS with the ALS Functional Rating Scale, and sum up progress as one number: how many points a person loses per month. But two people with the same average rate can follow very different paths. One declines steadily. Another stays fairly stable for a year, then worsens quickly. This team asked: does the shape of the curve tell us something the rate alone misses?

What they did. They used a population-based registry from the Piedmont region of Italy, covering people diagnosed from 2000 to 2022. Sixteen hundred eighty-six people had at least three rating-scale visits. The team grouped them two ways: once by speed of decline, and once by the shape of the curve over time. Then they checked the results in a second registry, from Emilia-Romagna, with seven hundred sixty-nine people.

What they found. Speed of decline was the stronger overall predictor of survival. But shape added useful detail. They found four shapes: a slow steady decline, in about forty-six percent; a plateau and then decline, in about twenty-three percent; a rapid early decline, in about sixteen percent; and a late decline group, about fifteen percent, or roughly one in seven. That late decline group looked similar to the slow steady group in the first year, then sped up. Their breathing tests, called forced vital capacity, were already lower at diagnosis, even though their overall scores looked reassuring. Their median survival was about twenty-three months, versus thirty-nine for the steady group. And within the same speed group, shape could separate survival by up to eighteen months. All four shapes showed up again in the second registry. The team also found that the fastest progressors are often left out of studies like this, because they don't live long enough to have three visits.

What it means. For people with sporadic ALS, this is a reminder that one number does not capture the whole picture. A moderate first year does not always mean a slow course. The authors suggest that regular breathing tests from diagnosis may give an early warning, even when the functional score looks fine. This is about planning care, not a new treatment.

Honest caveats. Both registries are from northern Italy. The groups are statistical patterns, not diagnoses, and the model needs several visits before it can sort anyone. It has not yet been tested prospectively in clinics. This summary is based on the full open-access paper.

What's next. The authors call for prospective testing, and note that clinical trials should account for both rate and shape, and for the fast progressors who are often missed. This is educational only, not medical advice. Talk with your care team about your own situation.

# Script: Where ALS starts and how fast it spreads — what motor patterns tell us

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published in the European Journal of Neurology on October 27, 2026, and it is open access. It comes from fifteen ALS centers in Germany and Austria, led by Thomas Meyer at Charité in Berlin. The title is: "Anatomically Defined Motor Phenotypes Reveal Dissociated Trajectories of Progression, Neurofilament Profiles, and Survival in ALS."

Some background. ALS looks very different from person to person. For some, it starts in speech or swallowing, for others in a hand or a leg. Some progress quickly, others slowly. This team asked: can a simple, structured way of describing where ALS starts, how fast it spreads, and which motor nerves are involved, explain some of that variation?

What did they do? They studied one thousand three hundred seven people with ALS. Neurologists sorted each person on three axes. First, where symptoms began. Second, whether symptoms spread to a distant body region, like from arm to leg, within the first twelve months. Third, the balance between upper motor neuron signs, like stiffness, and lower motor neuron signs, like weakness and muscle wasting. They compared these groups on progression rate, a blood marker of nerve damage called neurofilament light, and survival.

What they found. Different neurologists sorted people quite consistently. Early spread was the strongest warning sign: it went with faster progression, higher neurofilament, and almost three times the risk of death during follow-up. Onset in the breathing muscles of the trunk, though rare, progressed fastest. Speech or swallowing onset had the highest neurofilament levels, while onset near the hip or thigh had the slowest course. People with mostly upper motor neuron signs did better, and in the purely upper motor neuron group, there were no deaths during follow-up. Interestingly, neurofilament and functional decline did not always move together.

What it means. For people with sporadic ALS, this supports the idea that ALS is not one uniform disease. A simple bedside description of onset and spread carries real information about likely course. It may also help clinical trials compare like with like, and help interpret neurofilament results.

Honest caveats. Survival follow-up was short, and the patients came from specialist centers. Rare groups were small. Each of the three axes was tested separately, so we don't know how much each adds on its own. Thinking and behavior were not assessed. Some authors are linked to the company behind the app used to collect ratings. And these are group averages, not predictions for any one person. This summary is based on the full paper.

What's next? The authors call for longer, larger studies, and for using this classification in trial design. This is educational only, not medical advice. Talk with your care team about your own situation.

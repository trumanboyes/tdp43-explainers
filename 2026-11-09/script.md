# Script: Can a ketone drink ease the energy crisis in ALS?

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published in eClinicalMedicine in late October 2026, by a team led by Herrmann and Dorst at Ulm University in Germany. The title is: "Efficacy and tolerability of D-beta-hydroxybutyrate ester in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis," a trial called KETO-ALS.

The question. Many people with ALS burn more energy at rest than expected, which doctors call hypermetabolism, and weight loss is linked to faster progression. Ketone bodies are an alternative fuel the body normally makes during fasting. They can enter the brain and may give stressed nerve cells extra energy. Rather than a strict ketogenic diet, which is hard to follow with ALS, the team asked: does drinking a ketone supplement slow nerve damage?

What they did. This was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial at one center. Eighty-one people with ALS took either a ketone ester drink, 30 grams a day split into three doses, or a placebo drink, for six months, on top of riluzole and normal care. The main measure was the blood marker neurofilament light, or N-F-L, which rises when nerve fibers are damaged.

What they found. On the simple, pre-planned comparison, N-F-L dipped slightly in the ketone group and stayed flat on placebo, but the difference was not statistically significant. After statistically adjusting for differences between the groups, including higher starting N-F-L in the ketone group, the difference became significant. But a second, alternative analysis did not confirm it. Function scores declined faster in the ketone group in the unadjusted numbers, though not after adjustment, and there were no differences in weight, breathing, or survival. About seventy percent of participants were hypermetabolic. The drink was well tolerated, with no treatment-related serious side effects, though bad taste caused some dropouts.

What it means. This is a mixed, early signal, not proof of benefit. Ketone esters appear safe to study in ALS, and the authors see a possible effect on a nerve-damage marker, especially in people with higher starting N-F-L and those with better function at the start. But the clinical results did not show improvement, and the faster functional decline in the ketone group is a concern the authors themselves raise.

Honest caveats. One center, eighty-one people, six months. The groups were unbalanced at the start. The positive result depended on adjustments chosen after the trial. Subgroup findings are hypothesis-generating only.

What's next. The authors call for a larger, longer phase 3 trial focused on function and survival, and a better-tasting formula. If you're curious about ketone products, talk with your ALS care team first. Thanks for listening.

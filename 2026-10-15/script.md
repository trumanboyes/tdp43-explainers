# Script: A tiny piece of a nuclear-pore protein that fights TDP-43 clumping (NUP50)

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on October 6, 2026, in the journal Molecular Neurodegeneration. It comes from Wilfried Rossoll's lab at the Mayo Clinic in Jacksonville, Florida, led by first authors Courtney Smith and Bilal Khalil, working with Sami Barmada's team at the University of Michigan. The title is: "Nucleoporin 50 rescues hallmarks of TDP-43 proteinopathy in ALS/FTD disease models."

Here's the question. In nearly all ALS, including sporadic ALS, the protein TDP-43 leaves the cell's nucleus and forms sticky clumps outside it. Everything going in or out of the nucleus passes through tiny gates called nuclear pores, which are built from parts called nucleoporins. Traffic problems at these gates are well documented in ALS. But whether the gate parts themselves affect TDP-43 clumping wasn't well understood.

The team focused on one gate part, called NUP50, for a good reason. A 2023 genetics study linked NUP50 to ALS risk. Rare changes in the gene were more common in people with ALS and FTD, and lower NUP50 levels were tied to risk.

So what did they find? In cells grown in the lab, adding NUP50 reduced TDP-43 clumping. It lowered the sticky, hard-to-dissolve form of TDP-43, the visible clumps, and the extra chemical tags that mark diseased TDP-43. This protection only worked when NUP50 was attached to the nuclear pore. Then they narrowed it down. A tiny piece of NUP50, just 41 amino acids long, was enough to attach to the pore and reduce clumping. In neurons in a dish, that small piece reduced TDP-43 clumps and helped the neurons survive. An ALS-linked change in NUP50 worked less well against clumping. And in donated brain tissue from people who had ALS or FTD, NUP50 partly sat together with the TDP-43 clumps.

Why does this matter for sporadic ALS? It ties a genetic risk factor seen in ALS patients to the problem found in almost everyone with ALS: TDP-43 clumping. And a small fragment is an appealing starting point for drug design.

Now the honest caveats. The experiments were in cells and in neurons in a dish, plus donated brain tissue. The study summary reports no testing in living animals or people. Delivering a fragment like this into human motor neurons is still an open challenge. And three authors hold a patent on this work.

Next steps would be animal models, human motor neurons, and a delivery method. It's early, but it's a promising lead. That's tonight's paper. This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

# Script: TBK1 clears sticky TDP-43 monomers via interferon-beta and the immunoproteasome

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on October 5, 2026, in EMBO Reports. It comes from Koji Yamanaka's lab at Nagoya University, with first author Shohei Sakai and collaborators including Jemeen Sreedharan at King's College London. The title is: "TBK1 eliminates aggregation-prone monomeric TDP-43 through an IFNβ-immunoproteasome pathway in ALS models."

Here's the question. Mutations that weaken a protein called TBK1 cause ALS and FTD with TDP-43 pathology. This group's earlier work also found that active TBK1 is reduced in sporadic ALS tissue. TBK1 is already known for helping cells clear junk through autophagy, the cell's recycling system. But was that the whole story for TDP-43?

What did they do? They studied cells, human stem-cell-derived lower motor neurons, donated spinal cord tissue from people with ALS, and a mouse model of ALS. They asked whether TBK1 clears sticky, single-molecule forms of TDP-43 through a pathway that does not depend on autophagy.

What they found. Aggregation-prone monomeric TDP-43 physically associates with TBK1 and turns it on. Active TBK1 then induces interferon-beta, a signaling molecule. Interferon-beta ramps up the immunoproteasome, a specialized protein shredder, which clears monomeric TDP-43 in neurons, including human motor neurons grown from stem cells. Blocking autophagy did not stop this clearance. Separately, in spinal motor neurons from ALS patients with TDP-43 pathology, a receptor subunit for interferon-beta called IFNAR1 was downregulated, and it was worse in neurons that already had pathology. ER stress could lower IFNAR1. And in SOD1 mice, removing one copy of the Tbk1 gene impaired immunoproteasome induction and increased polyubiquitinated protein buildup at disease onset.

Why does this matter for sporadic ALS? Most people with ALS, including many gene-negative cases, have TDP-43 pathology and do not carry a TBK1 mutation. This work suggests a protective TBK1–interferon-beta–immunoproteasome axis that may be weakened in sporadic disease, not only in rare TBK1 mutation carriers. It is a mechanism story about how neurons might clear dangerous TDP-43 monomers — not a treatment trial, and not proof that boosting this pathway in people would help.

Honest caveats. This is cell, neuron, tissue, and mouse work. Interferon-beta itself was not directly measured in the mouse tissue. Whether the immunoproteasome stays protective across the full disease course is still open. And type I interferon signaling in ALS is context-dependent — other studies have painted a mixed picture — so this is not a green light to take interferon-beta.

Next steps would include clarifying when this pathway helps versus harms, measuring it more carefully in patient tissue, and asking whether carefully timed support of neuronal immunoproteasome function is even feasible. That's tonight's paper. This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

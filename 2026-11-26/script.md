# Script: Did calming the cell's stress alarm slow ALS? The ABBV-CLS-7262 HEALEY trial

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a peer-reviewed clinical trial report in JAMA Network Open, published online on November 2nd, 2026, and added to PubMed on November 23rd. It comes from Senda Ajroud-Driss, Sabrina Paganoni, Merit Cudkowicz, and many colleagues running the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial across the United States. The title is: "ABBV-CLS-7262 in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis: A Randomized Clinical Trial."

The question. When cells are under strain, they switch on an alarm system called the integrated stress response. It slows down protein making and helps form stress granules, which can trap TDP-43, the protein that clumps in most cases of ALS. A short burst of this alarm is protective, but if it stays on, it may harm motor neurons. ABBV-CLS-7262 is a pill, developed by AbbVie and Calico, that reaches the brain and turns this alarm down. The question was simple: does it slow ALS?

What they did. This was one arm, called regimen F, of the HEALEY platform trial, run at 74 U.S. centers between 2023 and 2024. Adults within three years of their first symptoms were randomly assigned to 120 milligrams or 240 milligrams of the drug once a day, or a placebo, for 24 weeks. In total, 234 people got the drug, and 126 got placebo, including placebo patients shared from another regimen. The main measure combined the ALS functional rating scale with survival.

What they found. The drug did not slow ALS. On the main measure, the result was almost exactly even with placebo. Function scores dropped about one point a month in both groups. Breathing, muscle strength, and quality of life were also no different. The drug did do what it was designed to do: in spinal fluid, a stress-response marker called FGF21 went down in both dose groups, but not on placebo. Side effects were similar to placebo, and no deaths were linked to the drug. One surprise: neurofilament light, a blood marker of nerve damage, rose about nine percent on the lower dose, which the authors call hypothesis-generating only.

What it means. This is a clear negative result. Turning down the stress response in people did not change the course of ALS, at least over six months. That is disappointing, but it is valuable: it closes one door quickly, and shows that hitting a target is not the same as helping patients.

Honest caveats. The trial lasted only 24 weeks, and people were up to three years from their first symptoms, which may be too late. Some subgroups might still respond, and the blood and spinal fluid markers may not fully reflect what happens inside motor neurons.

What's next. The HEALEY trial has since been redesigned to enroll people earlier and follow them longer, and the drug is being studied for other conditions. As always, this is educational only, and not medical advice.

# Script: Does a once-daily oral edaravone slow ALS? The ADORE phase 3 trial

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a peer-reviewed clinical trial report in The Lancet Neurology, in its December 2026 issue, first listed in PubMed on November 18th, 2026. It comes from Ruben van Eijk, Leonard van den Berg, and colleagues across Europe, in a trial funded by the drug company Ferrer and run with the TRICALS research network. The title is: "Safety and efficacy of daily oral edaravone, F-N-P one twenty-two, in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis: the ADORE trial."

The question. Edaravone is an antioxidant drug, a free-radical scavenger, that is already approved for ALS in several countries. Its approval rested on an earlier trial that showed slower decline over 24 weeks, but only in a carefully selected subset of patients. So this team asked: does a new once-a-day oral form of edaravone slow ALS in a broader group of people, over a longer time?

What they did. This was a large phase 3 trial at 37 hospitals in 12 countries. Adults with ALS who were within two years of their first symptoms, with fairly good breathing, and with disease moving at a moderate pace, were randomly assigned, two to one, to 100 milligrams of oral edaravone or a placebo every day for 48 weeks. Nobody, not the patients, the doctors, or the company, knew who got what. The main measure was the change in the ALS Functional Rating Scale, a 48-point score of everyday abilities. In all, 302 people were treated and analysed.

What they found. Over 48 weeks, both groups declined by about the same amount. Scores dropped by 12.6 points on edaravone and 13.4 points on placebo. That difference of less than one point was not statistically significant, and was well within chance. The drug was well tolerated, side effects were about as common as on placebo, and no deaths were linked to the treatment.

What it means. This is a clear and important result, even though it is disappointing. In this broader group of people, this oral form of edaravone, at this dose, did not slow ALS. The authors say the results do not support using it to change the course of the disease. A well-run negative trial is still valuable. It saves people time, money, and hope spent on something that does not work, and it pushes the field toward better ideas. One caution: this was a different formulation and a different trial design from the ones behind existing edaravone approvals, so it does not directly answer every question about those products. If you take edaravone now, talk with your care team before changing anything.

Honest caveats. We are working from the published summary here. The trial lasted 48 weeks, it mostly enrolled White patients in Europe, and nine people from one site that broke trial rules were left out of the analysis.

What's next. The authors call for more effective treatments, and many new approaches are now in trials. As always, this is educational only, and not medical advice.

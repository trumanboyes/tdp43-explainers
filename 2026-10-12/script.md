# Script: Fix one broken message (UNC13A and TDP-43)

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper comes from Pietro Fratta's team in London, and it was accepted by the journal Nature on October 6, 2026. The title is: "UNC13A splice correction rescues synaptic dysfunction induced by TDP-43 loss." Let's unpack that in plain English.

First, some background. Inside almost every nerve cell, a protein called TDP-43 works like an editor in the cell's control room, the nucleus. Genes get copied into working instructions, and TDP-43 helps cut out junk pieces before those instructions are used. In about 97 out of every 100 people with ALS, TDP-43 leaves the nucleus and clumps up outside it. So the editor is missing from its desk.

When that happens, junk pieces, which scientists call cryptic exons, get left in some of the instructions. One of the most important damaged instructions is for a protein called UNC13A. It sits at the synapse, the tiny gap where one nerve cell passes a message to the next, and its job is to get little packets of chemical messenger ready to release. Without it, the message mostly doesn't get sent. Common variations in this gene were already known to raise ALS risk and speed up progression, so it was a strong suspect.

So what did this study find? The team grew human nerve cells, including spinal motor neurons, from stem cells, and turned TDP-43 down. The cells still looked healthy under the microscope. But their communication broke down. Fewer messages were released, and the network stopped firing in sync. The researchers call this a pre-degenerative state: the wiring is still there, but the signal is failing.

Here's the striking part. Fixing just that one error, out of hundreds caused by losing TDP-43, was enough to restore communication. They did it two ways: by cutting the junk piece out of the gene, and with antisense oligonucleotides. Those are short, drug-like strands of genetic material that stick to the instructions and steer the editing back to normal. They worked in human neurons, and in mice carrying the human version of the gene.

Why does this matter? It suggests some ALS weakness may come from nerve cells that are still alive, but not talking well. And that could be fixable. A company co-founded by the senior author, Trace Neuroscience, is now testing a drug based on this idea, called TRCN-1023, in early trials in Europe and China.

Now the caveats. This is lab and mouse work, not people. The trials so far are mainly testing safety, so we won't know for a while whether it helps patients. And the authors have a financial stake in the drug.

Still, the takeaway is hopeful. A single, fixable messaging problem may sit near the heart of most ALS. That's tonight's paper. Thanks for listening.

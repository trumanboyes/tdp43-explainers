# Script: Donor-aware reanalysis links nuclear TDP-43 loss to STMN2 depletion

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper is a bioRxiv preprint posted November 3, 2026, from Abdullah Chaudhary and Saiqi Yao at Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology. The title is: "Donor Aware Reanalysis of Neuronal Nuclei Supports STMN2 Depletion Associated with Nuclear TDP-43 Loss in FTD and ALS."

The question. In most ALS and in many cases of frontotemporal dementia, the protein TDP-43 leaves the cell nucleus. When that happens, genes that TDP-43 normally keeps in check can go wrong. Two of the best-studied are STMN2, which helps axons grow and repair, and UNC13A, involved in how nerve cells talk to each other. But small postmortem studies can be muddied by differences between donors. This team asked: if you carefully account for who each sample came from, does nuclear TDP-43 loss still clearly link to lower STMN2?

What they did. They reanalyzed a public RNA-sequencing dataset of neuronal nuclei sorted from frontal cortex, originally published as GSE126542. The matrix covered about sixty-three thousand genes across twelve libraries from seven donors, with five complete matched pairs: nuclei with nuclear TDP-43 retained versus lost from the same person. They used a primary statistical model that included both donor identity and nuclear TDP-43 status, plus two sensitivity checks.

What they found. In the donor-aware model, three thousand four hundred forty-two genes differed at a false-discovery rate below five percent: about eighteen hundred sixty-five lower and fifteen hundred seventy-seven higher when nuclear TDP-43 was lost. STMN2 stood out. Its expression was about seventy-one point five percent lower in the loss nuclei, with a very strong statistical signal, and it was lower in every one of the five complete donor pairs. TARDBP, the gene that encodes TDP-43 itself, was also moderately lower. UNC13A whole-gene counts were modestly higher in the main model, but that signal disappeared in the sensitivity checks. The authors stress that a whole-gene count cannot tell you whether a cryptic exon is being included.

What it means. Nuclear TDP-43 loss is the common pathology in roughly ninety-seven percent of ALS, including most people without a known gene mutation. This reanalysis adds careful statistical support that STMN2 depletion travels with that nuclear loss in human neuronal nuclei. It is not a new treatment, and it does not by itself prove the splicing mechanism.

Honest caveats. This is a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed. The cohort is small, and the source tissue was from people with FTD or FTD-ALS carrying a C9orf72 expansion, not from sporadic ALS alone. There were no healthy controls, and no splice-junction, isoform, or protein measurements. Mechanistic claims still need junction-level validation. This summary is based on the full preprint PDF.

What's next. The authors call for reprocessing all eligible libraries from the raw files, junction-aware and long-read RNA work, and independent cohorts. This is educational only, not medical advice. Talk with your care team about your own situation.

# Script: Does ALS whisper before it speaks? Early warning signs in mostly sporadic ALS

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on October 9, 2026, in the journal eBioMedicine. It comes from twenty ALS centres in Germany and Switzerland, led by Isabell Cordts and Paul Lingor at the Technical University of Munich. The title is: "EARLY-ALS: a multicentre study on prodromal amyotrophic lateral sclerosis."

Here's the question. In diseases like Parkinson's and Alzheimer's, doctors know there's a quiet early phase, sometimes years long, before the disease is obvious. Is the same true for ALS? So far, most of the evidence came from families with inherited ALS. This study asked about sporadic ALS, the kind most people have.

What did they do? Four hundred seventy-five people with ALS, only about four percent with a known genetic cause, filled out a detailed online questionnaire. So did two hundred eighty-five comparison people, mostly spouses, relatives and friends. It asked about the ten years before symptoms began: complaints, doctor visits, other illnesses, work, habits, and weight.

What they found. When asked an open question about changes before their ALS began, people with ALS were far more likely to describe something: about one in four did, compared with one in twenty controls. Most of it was subtle motor trouble, like mild weakness, cramps and trouble walking, plus pain, especially back and limb pain, often a year or two before recognised onset. Trembling of the arms or legs was the single most reported early sign. People with ALS also saw neurologists more often in the year before onset. Herniated discs were about twice as common, mostly five or more years earlier. Physically demanding jobs and lower school leaving levels were more common too. And weight: men with ALS started losing weight before their first symptoms, and on average, people with ALS weighed about six kilos, roughly fourteen pounds, less than controls by the time of the survey.

Why does this matter for sporadic ALS? It supports the idea that ALS doesn't start on the day the first symptom is noticed. There may be an earlier window of mild motor changes. That could one day help doctors diagnose ALS sooner, and start treatments and trials earlier, while more motor neurons are still working. The weight finding also backs up something your care team likely already stresses: keeping weight and calories up matters in ALS.

Honest caveats. This was a look back, relying on people's memories after diagnosis, so recall bias is a big concern. The controls were mostly family members, there were no blood tests, scans or nerve tests to confirm anything, and many comparisons were made, so some findings could be chance. It doesn't show that discs, jobs or habits cause ALS, and none of these signs can predict ALS in a healthy person.

What's next? Following people forward in time, with nerve tests and blood markers like neurofilament, to see whether these early signs truly come first. That's tonight's paper. This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.

# Script: Lowering ataxin-2 switches on backup energy and support systems against TDP-43

*Narrated by ElevenLabs (voice: Carolyn). Plain-English explainer, not medical advice.*

Hi. Tonight's paper was published on October 15, 2026, in Cell Reports. It comes from Ana Jovičić's team at Genentech in South San Francisco, with first author Denise Gastaldo. The title is: "Ataxin-2 downregulation rewires neuronal and astrocytic metabolism to protect against TDP-43 toxicity."

Here's the question. In most people with ALS, including sporadic, gene-negative ALS, a protein called TDP-43 goes wrong. It leaves the nucleus and clumps up. For years, scientists have known that lowering another protein, ataxin-2, protects yeast, flies, and mice from TDP-43 damage. An antisense drug to lower it reached a clinical trial, but the trial stopped without benefit, possibly because ataxin-2 wasn't lowered enough. So how does lowering ataxin-2 actually protect cells?

What did they do? They measured thousands of proteins in yeast that make human TDP-43, and in brain tissue from a TDP-43 mouse model treated with an ataxin-2-lowering antisense drug. Then they tested the leads in mouse neurons grown in dishes, alone or with support cells called astrocytes.

What they found. Lowering ataxin-2 didn't simply undo TDP-43 damage; it switched on backup programs. In neurons, it boosted glycolysis, a way of making energy that doesn't depend on the damaged mitochondria, the cell's power plants, with help from an enzyme called I-D-H-1. That recovered roughly forty to fifty percent of the lost energy production. Blocking I-D-H-1 erased the benefit, and extra I-D-H-1 alone helped neurons survive. In astrocytes, lowering ataxin-2 ramped up cholesterol production through an enzyme called H-M-G-C-S-1, and sending that cholesterol out to neurons was needed for protection. Neurons were fully rescued only when both cell types were treated together. Lowering ataxin-2 also partly protected neurons that had lost TDP-43, not just ones with too much. And in healthy mice, it changed very few proteins.

Why does this matter for sporadic ALS? TDP-43 problems are found in most sporadic ALS. The authors suggest ataxin-2 drugs may need very deep lowering to work; in their experiments it was more than ninety percent. They also point to I-D-H-1 and astrocyte cholesterol as possible new drug targets.

Honest caveats. This is yeast, mice, and mouse cells in dishes, not people. The mice overproduce TDP-43, which isn't how sporadic ALS starts. The energy and cholesterol pathways weren't tested directly in living animals. And no diet was tested, so this says nothing about eating more sugar or cholesterol.

What's next? Testing these pathways in animals and other ALS models, and using them to check whether future ataxin-2 drugs are working. That's tonight's paper. This is educational, not medical advice. Thanks for listening.
